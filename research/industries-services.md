# Service industries, work organization and cross-sector exposures

Research snapshot: October 22, 2026. Purpose: reusable evidence for future Iowa cancer questions and data/model work. This supplements the manufacturing, food/ag-processing and energy/legacy memos. A hazardous agent, a task, an occupation, an industry and a community exposure are different variables.

## Healthcare, laboratories and pharmacies

NIOSH identifies occupational exposure opportunities during preparation/administration of hazardous drugs, surface cleaning, waste handling, disinfection and specimen preservation. Its 2024 hazardous-drug list is a starting point for identifying agents, not a list proving that every worker receives a carcinogenic dose. Not every hazardous drug has the same cancer endpoint. Source: https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/docs/2025-103/ ; https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/healthcare/risk-factors/chemical-hazards.html ; https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/healthcare/risk-factors/antineoplastic-agents.html .

Blair et al. (2001) conducted an Iowa/Minnesota population-based leukemia case-control study with 513 cases and 1,087 controls, lifetime job histories and a job-exposure matrix. Its abstract reports increased leukemia risk among nursing/healthcare workers, agricultural services and several solvent-related jobs; some food/metal/painter/printer findings were nonsignificant. It is historical, exploratory occupational evidence, not an estimate of current hospital risk or proof of a specific drug's causal effect. The full occupational exposure pattern and subtype matter. Original: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11439392/ ; doi:10.1002/ajim.1066.

For radiation work, the U.S. Radiologic Technologists cohort provides an example of stronger exposure reconstruction: individual work history and organ/tissue dosimetry rather than an industry label alone. It began with 146,022 certified technologists and is a national cohort, not an Iowa-specific estimate. Future Iowa models would need actual historical dosimetry/task records before assigning comparable doses. Official study description: https://dceg.cancer.gov/research/who-we-study/cohorts/us-radiologic-technologists .

## Funeral services and pathology

An unusually specific Iowa historical record exists. OSHA's December 12, 1986 Federal Register discussion summarizes Iowa Health Department/University Hygienic Laboratory sampling in 44 randomly chosen funeral homes during 1982-1983. Formaldehyde exposure during embalming ranged from nondetectable to 2.98 ppm, mean 0.48 ppm. One reported eight-hour average was 1.62 ppm. These are historical measurements summarized in an official rulemaking record; the underlying 18-page study was not independently obtained here. They are not current measurements and not cancer outcome counts. Official record: https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-1986-12-12/pdf/FR-1986-12-12.pdf , printed page 44797.

NCI's current formaldehyde overview identifies higher-exposure occupations and established cancer hazards. A potential model needs embalming/specimen tasks, dates, ventilation and measured concentration, with appropriate duration/peak metrics. Do not transfer a mortuary task's concentration to office staff, a hospital's entire workforce or nearby residents. https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/formaldehyde .

## Cleaning, dry cleaning and maintenance

The Iowa bladder case-control study (1,452 cases; 2,434 controls) reported occupational associations for several jobs including mechanics, machine operators and some cleaning/laundry work. Different jobs involve different solvents and routes; the job title does not identify a chemical dose. Original: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/12134533/ .

Dry cleaning needs separate historical solvent and community-pathway records. The energy/legacy memo contains the June 2026 Atlantic PCE assessment and other site documents. A current residence near a former dry cleaner is not equivalent to past employment inside one. Preserve PCE versus TCE, indoor vapor versus groundwater versus finished drinking water, actual sampling years and the assessor's conclusion.

The Iowa/Minnesota leukemia study also reported janitor/cleaner and light-truck-driver associations. These should be treated as hypotheses informed by tasks and exposure history, with multiple comparisons and confounding considered. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11439392/ .

## Salons and barbers

IARC's occupational hairdresser/barber assessment concerns work involving mixtures and historical product formulations. NCI distinguishes occupational exposure from personal hair-product use and describes uncertainty across outcomes. It cannot be used to label every contemporary salon product carcinogenic or assign a risk multiplier to every Iowa cosmetologist. https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/hair-dyes-fact-sheet ; https://publications.iarc.fr/_publications/media/download/2976/5e2077d5281b08ffc7705aa8887258e20f6b50d6.pdf .

A pooled InterLymph original analysis also examined occupations including hairdressers, cleaners, spray painters, electrical workers and carpenters. Its associations are useful for hypothesis generation and task-based occupational coding, not for estimating Iowa's cancer excess without population-specific exposure data. https://www.iarc.who.int/reference/occupation-and-risk-of-non-hodgkin-lymphoma-and-its-subtypes-a-pooled-analysis-from-the-interlymph-consortium/ .

## Night work across industries

IARC classifies night shift work as probably carcinogenic (Group 2A), based on the evaluation of circadian-disrupting work patterns and a combination of human, animal and mechanistic evidence. It is not a diagnosis-specific risk estimate for every worker who sometimes works late. Hospitals, transportation, manufacturing and hospitality can share the exposure. https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-monographs-evaluation-of-the-carcinogenicity-of-night-shift-work/ ; https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/QA_Monographs_Volume124.pdf .

For a future model, collect schedule timing, rotation, frequency and duration rather than coding all employment in a 24-hour industry as exposed. An Iowa-specific current attributable fraction was not identified in this review.

## Product consumption is a separate pathway

Breweries, distilleries, food manufacturers and retail outlets can matter through products consumed by the population as well as through worker/community emissions. Alcohol consumption is a causal cancer risk; a county's brewery employment or production is not a direct measure of residents' alcohol intake. Source: https://www.cdc.gov/cancer/risk-factors/alcohol.html .

Likewise, evidence concerning eating processed meat is not the same as evidence about employment in meatpacking. The food/ag-processing memo preserves the separate worker studies and exposure measurements. Models must declare whether they study production emissions, occupational exposure, product consumption or access/marketing before selecting a proxy.

## Modeling notes specific to this memo

- Several Iowa occupation papers reuse the same underlying controls or surveillance periods. Do not treat overlapping populations as independent replications or combine their odds ratios without accounting for overlap.
- Occupation at death, current job, longest job and lifetime job histories are different exposure variables. SOC occupation, NAICS industry and a job-exposure matrix cannot be interchanged without a documented crosswalk.
- Employment counts describe jobs/establishments, not dose. Residents may commute to another county; retirees may have moved before diagnosis.
- A service-sector absence from TRI is not absence of exposure. Reporting thresholds and covered sectors omit many small establishments and mobile tasks.
- Retain null and nonsignificant findings, confidence intervals and historical periods. No current Iowa industry-specific causal attributable fraction is derived here.
