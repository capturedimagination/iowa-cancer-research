# Iowa cancer: environmental, agricultural and occupational evidence

Research cutoff/access date: October 22, 2026. This memo distinguishes original studies from reviews and hazard classifications from measured individual risk. Estimates below are relative associations, not absolute lifetime risks or proof that an exposure caused an individual's cancer. HR = hazard ratio; RR = rate ratio; CI = confidence interval. A CI spanning 1 includes no association under the model. Multiple outcomes/subgroups raise chance-finding concerns. Long cancer latency means present exposures cannot explain every present diagnosis.

## Main assessment

Radon is an established lung carcinogen with unusually prevalent Iowa household exposure and practical mitigation. Some specific agricultural chemicals and water contaminants have credible cancer associations, including studies of Iowa residents, but neither county maps, chemical-use totals nor any single study establishes the proportion of Iowa's statewide excess caused by agriculture. Broad farmer-versus-general-population comparisons also do not establish pesticide safety: different smoking, activity, screening and selection can lower total cancer while some specific cancers increase. Root report should combine this with the September 2026 UI/HHS Key Drivers report, rather than present the environmental literature as a competing all-or-nothing explanation.

## Drinking-water nitrate: definitions and evidence

The US nitrate limit is **10 mg/L as nitrogen (NO3-N)**; nitrite has a separate 1 mg/L limit. Nitrate as nitrogen and nitrate ion are not interchangeable: 10 mg/L NO3-N is about 44.3 mg/L nitrate ion (molecular-weight conversion). Study concentrations below are NO3-N. The regulatory limit chiefly addresses infant methemoglobinemia; meeting it is a compliance finding, not proof of zero cancer risk over decades. Conversely, an observational association below 10 is not an established replacement legal threshold. Vegetable nitrate, water nitrate, and processed-meat nitrite have different accompanying nutrients/precursors and should not be conflated.

Primary regulatory/practical sources:
- Iowa HHS, [Nitrate Testing Results in Public Water](https://hhs.iowa.gov/about/data-reports/environment/drinking-water/nitrate).
- EPA, [Frequently Asked Questions About Nitrate and Drinking Water](https://nepis.epa.gov/Exe/ZyPURL.cgi?Dockey=P10150PM.TXT): boiling does not reduce nitrate; evaporation can concentrate it. Ordinary disinfection is not nitrate treatment.
- EPA, [What should I do if water exceeds the nitrate MCL?](https://www.epa.gov/mn/what-should-i-do-if-my-water-has-elevated-nitrates-andor-exceeds-mcl): use an alternative safe source above the limit; suitable reverse osmosis/distillation are options. Select treatment for the tested contaminant and verify effectiveness.

### Key original studies (Iowa-specific or Iowa-containing cohorts)

1. **Ovarian cancer, Iowa Women's Health Study (IWHS), Inoue-Choi et al., 2015** (online December 8, 2014), Int J Cancer 137:173–182. Among 28,555 postmenopausal women, 315 ovarian cancers through 2010. Highest public-water NO3-N quartile (≥2.98 mg/L) versus lowest (≤0.47): **HR 2.03, 95% CI 1.22–3.38**, trend p=.003, multivariable adjusted; accounting for THMs did not explain it. Private-well users: HR 1.53 (0.93–2.54), not statistically significant. Historical utility estimates and home-source questionnaire are imperfect individual exposure measures. This is not evidence that every Iowan drinking 3 mg/L doubles cancer risk. [PubMed/original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/25430487/), DOI 10.1002/ijc.29365.

2. **Bladder cancer, IWHS, Jones et al., 2016**, Environmental Health Perspectives 124:1751–1758. Cohort 34,708; public-supply analysis 15,577 women using same supply >10 years; 130 bladder cancers in that subset. ≥4 years with NO3-N >5 mg/L versus zero years: **HR 1.62 (1.06–2.47)**, trend p=.03. Highest versus lowest average-concentration quartile: **HR 1.48 (0.92–2.40)**, not statistically significant, trend p=.11. Models included age, smoking and smoking pack-years; additional THM adjustment changed little. Different exposure metrics gave different certainty. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27258851/), DOI 10.1289/EHP191.

3. **Kidney cancer, IWHS, Jones et al., 2017**, Epidemiology 28:703–711. Public-supply subset 15,577; 125 kidney cancers. Highest 5% of average NO3-N versus lowest quartile: **HR 2.3 (1.2–4.3)**, but **no monotonic trend** (p=.33); the full paper describes association at average >5 mg/L. Lower categories were not consistently elevated. No independent association with the studied DBPs; no increased risk for private-well use versus lowest public-water nitrate group. Adjusted analyses included smoking and BMI; residual confounding/unmeasured contaminants remain possible. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28252454/), [full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5538922/), DOI 10.1097/EDE.0000000000000647.

4. **Ovarian cancer, Agricultural Health Study (AHS), Ammons et al., 2025**, Environmental Epidemiology 9:e382 (May 13 online; June issue). Iowa/NC women, N=33,689; 166 ovarian cancers through 2021. Private-well nitrate modeled; public-water nitrate from monitoring. Per 5 mg/L water nitrate-N: **HR 1.15 (0.96–1.39)**, an imprecise positive association not statistically significant. THMs per 37 µg/L: HR 1.06 (1.02–1.10). Models adjusted age, BMI, smoking, menopausal status. No statistical nitrate interaction with vitamin C/red meat. The water-nitrate result is weaker than the earlier IWHS result, and must not be described as definitive confirmation. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40370528/), DOI 10.1097/EE9.0000000000000382.

5. **Prostate cancer, AHS, Spaur et al., 2026**, JNCI (April 1 issue; PMID 41423865). N=40,403 initially cancer-free men, follow-up through 2021 (mean 21.9 years); 3,625 prostate cancers, 2,200 aggressive. >10 mg/L average nitrate-N versus ≤1: total **HR 1.16 (1.01–1.35)**; aggressive **HR 1.22 (1.02–1.47)**. Highest versus lowest quartile estimates were weaker: total 1.05 (0.95–1.16), aggressive 1.13 (0.99–1.27). No association with total THMs/HAAs. Six percent had modeled/estimated average nitrate above 10. This is useful recent evidence at **above-limit** nitrate, not a general finding that low-level nitrate explains Iowa prostate incidence. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41423865/), DOI 10.1093/jnci/djaf350.

Balanced context: NCI's [Drinking Water Contaminants research summary](https://dceg.cancer.gov/research/what-we-study/drinking-water-contaminants) reports IWHS positive associations for thyroid, ovarian, bladder and kidney cancers, but **no association with water nitrate for colorectal or pancreatic cancer**. Iowa/Nebraska case-control studies observed higher colon/kidney/stomach risk in combinations of high water nitrate and high meat intake. These are heterogeneous cancer-specific results. Nitrate can form N-nitroso compounds under favorable conditions; plausibility does not itself quantify human risk. NCI also describes Iowa DBP associations with rectal/bladder cancer over long exposure periods; do not advise abandoning disinfection, which prevents serious infectious disease.

## Public water versus private wells

The June 2026 [Iowa DNR 2025 Annual Compliance Report](https://www.iowadnr.gov/media/9746/download?inline=), printed p.1 and p.21 (PDF pages 4 and 24), lists 1,815 systems. **97.36% of systems and 98.91% of the population served by systems met health-based standards**. There were **36 nitrate-nitrogen violations at 13 public systems**, listed system population 2,479. This is a count of violations, not 36 towns, and does not estimate lifetime cancer burden. River/source-water measurements differ from treated finished water. The report's no-reported-waterborne-illness statement does not rule out long-latency cancer associations. The report contains inconsistent population totals/percentages in some prose, so use these clearly labeled compliance measures instead of deriving the state's private-well share from them.

Private wells are not subject to mandated public-system water-quality standards. Iowa DNR's live [Well Testing guidance](https://www.iowadnr.gov/environmental-protection/water-quality/private-well-program/well-testing) recommends **annual bacteria and nitrate tests**, arsenic and manganese **every three years** in the funded testing section. It also recommends at least one arsenic test elsewhere on the same page; the recurring three-year schedule is the more specific current program guidance. Contact county environmental health for free testing through the Private Well Grants program; expanded tests including PFAS require approval. Construction, geology, well depth and surrounding land uses matter; depth alone does not certify safety. Test again after floods/repairs/changes and after treatment installation as advised. Use certified labs.

## Radon: strong evidence and high Iowa relevance

Iowa HHS [Radon Resources](https://hhs.iowa.gov/health-prevention/providers-professionals/radiological-health/radon/radon-resources) cites the Iowa survey finding **71.6% of homes above 4 pCi/L** and describes Iowa as entirely EPA Zone 1. This is a historical survey estimate, not a 2026 census of every current house, and does not predict any one home's level. Radon is generated naturally in soil/rock and can accumulate indoors.

The original **Iowa Radon Lung Cancer Study**, Field et al., AJE 2000;151:1091–1102, included 413 female lung-cancer cases and 614 controls who occupied their current homes ≥20 years. It adjusted for age, smoking, education. The categorical all-cases model found excess odds **0.50 (0.004–1.81)** per exposure approximately corresponding to 4 pCi/L over the relevant period; uncertainty varied by model. This adds Iowa-specific individual-level evidence to the established international causal evidence. [Journal/original](https://academic.oup.com/aje/article-abstract/151/11/1091/87328), DOI 10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a010153.

[EPA action guidance](https://www.epa.gov/radon/what-epas-action-level-radon-and-what-does-it-mean): fix at ≥4 pCi/L, consider fixing at 2–4; no known safe threshold. Test homes regardless of county, building age or neighbor results; arrange proper follow-up and credentialed mitigation, then retest. Smoking and radon together create particularly high lung-cancer risk.

Current Iowa policy: HHS says passive radon control became required for new single-/two-family construction **beginning July 1, 2026**; state code applies statewide to qualifying new construction. Existing real-estate transactions require disclosure of known results but no statewide mandatory test/mitigation. [Same HHS page](https://hhs.iowa.gov/health-prevention/providers-professionals/radiological-health/radon/radon-resources). Public schools must test each attendance center by **July 1, 2027**, then at least every five years, post results and follow mitigation requirements: [HHS schools guidance](https://hhs.iowa.gov/health-prevention/providers-professionals/radiological-health/radon/radon-k-12-schools). Licensed childcare centers have testing/mitigation requirements as detailed on HHS resources page.

## Pesticides: ingredient-specific evidence

**Glyphosate:** IARC classifies it Group 2A (probably carcinogenic), based on combined human/animal/mechanistic evidence. The large prospective **Andreotti et al., JNCI 2018** AHS analysis (54,251 applicators; follow-up to Iowa 2013/NC 2012) found no association with solid tumors or lymphoid malignancies including NHL. Highest quartile versus never-use: total cancer RR 0.99 (0.91–1.08); NHL 0.87 (0.64–1.20). AML was elevated but imprecise in primary analysis: **RR 2.44 (0.94–6.32)**; one 20-year-lag analysis was statistically significant (2.04, 1.05–3.97). Authors called for confirmation. Exposure self-report, imputation, latency and small subtype counts limit certainty. [Original](https://academic.oup.com/jnci/article/110/5/509/4590280), [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6279255/), DOI 10.1093/jnci/djx233.

EPA's live [Glyphosate page](https://www.epa.gov/ingredients-used-pesticide-products/glyphosate) retains its prior not-likely-carcinogenic scientific position, while acknowledging the 2022 court vacatur of the human-health portion and EPA withdrawal of its 2020 interim registration decision. A revised human-health/cancer evaluation was anticipated **late 2026**, with an August 2026 literature review and September 24 comment deadline. **Do not claim the new reassessment is complete as of October 22.** Regulatory permission and IARC hazard classification answer different questions; the disagreement is substantive and not resolved by a verdict or settlement.

**Atrazine:** Remigio et al., EHP 2024, 53,562 Iowa/NC applicators and 8,915 cancers: ever-use linked to lung cancer **RR 1.24 (1.04–1.46)**. Aggressive prostate highest-exposure quartile overall was **1.20 (0.95–1.52)**, not statistically significant; stronger under-60 subgroup **3.04 (1.61–5.75)**. Under-50 ever-use NHL estimate 2.43 (1.10–5.38), with weak age-interaction evidence. Subgroup results require caution. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38381478/), DOI 10.1289/EHP13684.

**Critical current classification change:** on **November 21, 2025**, IARC upgraded **atrazine and alachlor to Group 2A**. Atrazine human evidence included t(14;18)-positive NHL; alachlor included laryngeal cancer. Vinclozolin became Group 2B. Old government summaries still showing atrazine Group 3 are outdated. [IARC evaluation notice](https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-monographs-evaluation-of-the-carcinogenicity-of-atrazine-alachlor-and-vinclozolin/), DOI 10.1016/S1470-2045(25)00702-8.

**Dicamba:** Lerro et al., IJE 2020, 49,922 applicators. Highest-use quartile linked to liver/intrahepatic bile duct cancer **RR 1.80 (1.26–2.56)**, based on 28 exposed cases; persisted under lagged analyses. Earlier lung/colon signals disappeared with longer follow-up. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32357211/), DOI 10.1093/ije/dyaa066. This illustrates both a concern and the need to update older claims.

**Other insecticides:** Koutros et al., AJE 2013 reported aggressive-prostate associations with fonofos, malathion, terbufos and aldrin, but noted multiple testing and limited mechanistic evidence. Some chemicals are no longer registered and historical exposure remains relevant. [Original](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3590039/), [NIH study update](https://aghealth.nih.gov/news/2014.html). IARC classifies 2,4-D Group 2B, based on inadequate human and limited animal evidence: [IARC evaluation](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/books/NBK507409/?report=reader).

**Mechanistic versus cancer endpoints:** a 2023 AHS biomarker study linked urinary glyphosate to oxidative-stress markers in 268 farmers/100 nonfarmers. This is evidence about mechanisms, not a cancer diagnosis or an individual clinical prediction: [original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36629488/), DOI 10.1093/jnci/djac242. The larger cancer cohort and mechanistic findings can coexist.

**County pesticide maps and smoking comparisons:** [Gerken et al., Frontiers in Cancer Control and Society 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fcacs.2024.1368086) modeled county pesticide-use profiles and cancer incidence. It is ecological: it does not measure which individuals were exposed, nor establish that living in an Iowa agricultural county carries the same causal or absolute risk as smoking. Chemical-use mass is not absorbed dose; mixtures, correlated farming factors and exposure timing complicate inference.

Practical response: use label-required controls/PPE, trained application, drift reduction, safe storage, hygiene and measures preventing take-home contamination; individual exposure prevention need not await an exact statewide attributable fraction. Avoid blanket claims that all pesticides have the same cancer evidence.

## PFAS

IARC's November 2023 evaluation classified **PFOA Group 1** and **PFOS Group 2B**. The Group 1 assessment combined sufficient animal evidence and strong mechanistic evidence in exposed humans; human kidney/testicular evidence was limited. Classification does not mean every PFAS is equally carcinogenic or that any detectable amount guarantees cancer. [IARC primary evaluation announcement](https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-monographs-evaluate-the-carcinogenicity-of-perfluorooctanoic-acid-pfoa-and-perfluorooctanesulfonic-acid-pfos.).

Iowa DNR's March 3, 2023 report on 2021–2022 targeted sampling (PDF p.5) found ≥1 PFAS in **52/126 finished-water samples (41%)**, representing 116 public supplies. Sites were chosen for susceptibility/potential sources, **not a random sample of Iowa taps**. Comparisons in that historical report use 2022 health advisories, not the 2024 MCL; do not relabel them current violations. Detections prompted further monitoring/changes. [Original DNR report](https://www.iowadnr.gov/media/7038/download?inline=).

The 2024 federal rule set **4 ppt (ng/L) each for PFOA and PFOS**. On **May 18, 2026 EPA proposed**, rather than finalized, an option for eligible systems to request two extra years to 2031 for those limits, alongside a separate proposal rescinding/reconsidering other PFAS provisions. Do not say every deadline was already extended or all other limits repealed. [EPA proposed extension page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/proposed-pfoa-and-pfos-compliance-extension-rule), [EPA PFAS drinking-water rule page](https://www.epa.gov/sdwa/and-polyfluoroalkyl-substances-pfas?_hsmi=302130317).

Iowa private-well PFAS has been detected even in some wells ~300 ft deep. County-approved testing may be free; private wells are not subject to public-system enforceable limits. Suitable GAC, anion exchange, RO can reduce PFAS; effectiveness depends on product/chemical/maintenance. [DNR PFAS and Private Wells, 2-page guidance](https://www.iowadnr.gov/media/4619/download?inline=), [EPA certified filter guidance](https://www.epa.gov/water-research/identifying-drinking-water-filters-certified-reduce-pfas). No study reviewed establishes how much of Iowa's statewide excess is attributable to PFAS.

## Animal feeding operations (AFO/CAFO)

**New 2026 ecological study:** Son, Deziel, Bell, Environmental Research 299:124298 (online March 17, 2026; June 15 issue), analyzed county cancer incidence 2000–2021 in CA, IA, TX using propensity matching. High-density Iowa counties versus matched controls had all-cancer **IRR 1.079 (1.066–1.091)** (~7.9% higher); colorectal associations were noted. This is county-level association, not a measured personal risk from living a particular distance from a hog barn. Aggregate smoking/demographic adjustment, facility-density exposure, historical changes and unmeasured factors limit causal inference. [Original abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/41856233/), DOI 10.1016/j.envres.2026.124298.

**Iowa individual-level AHS study, 2020:** Jones et al., Epidemiology 31:478–489, evaluated 32,635 applicators/nearby animal intensity. Associations with blood/lymph cancers within 5 km were strongest in intermediate exposure categories (e.g. fourth quintile total lymphohematopoietic **HR 1.61, 1.21–2.14**), **without monotonic trend**. No clear associations within 2 km or among spouses. Adjusted age, BMI, education, smoking, alcohol, applicator type, family history; pesticide adjustment did not change results. AFO location is a proxy for mixed exposures and cannot identify the responsible pollutant. [Original](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/32168021/), [full paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7889404/). This is more nuanced than saying all nearby residents face a proven fixed increase.

## Other environmental and occupational risks

- **Arsenic:** naturally occurring groundwater contamination is relevant to Iowa wells. Chronic inorganic arsenic is an established carcinogen; exposure must be measured. Public-water MCL is 10 µg/L, but private-well users need testing. Do not presume agricultural origin. [DNR testing guidance](https://www.iowadnr.gov/environmental-protection/water-quality/private-well-program/well-testing), [NCI arsenic/water research](https://dceg.cancer.gov/research/what-we-study/drinking-water-contaminants).
- **Air/diesel:** IARC classifies outdoor air pollution/particulate matter and diesel exhaust as Group 1, with strong lung-cancer evidence. These hazards apply to Iowa but reviewed sources do not calculate an Iowa-specific excess-cancer share. [IARC outdoor-air Q&A](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/pr221_QA.pdf), [diesel evaluation](https://www.iarc.who.int/news-events/iarc-diesel-engine-exhaust-carcinogenic/).
- **Workplace agents:** asbestos, benzene, respirable crystalline silica, formaldehyde, some metals/solvents and wood dust are recognized hazards relevant to construction, demolition, manufacturing and other work. Total state rankings do not identify individual exposure. [NCI known environmental carcinogens](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances?cid=soc_fb_en_enterprise_nca50). Iowa DNR warns asbestos can affect demolition/training fires and requires appropriate certified work: [Iowa DNR asbestos](https://www.iowadnr.gov/environmental-protection/air-quality/asbestos-training-fires-controlled-burns).
- **Firefighting:** IARC Group 1 occupational circumstance, sufficient human evidence for mesothelioma/bladder, limited for colon/prostate/testis/melanoma/NHL. [IARC July 1, 2022 evaluation](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/pr317_E.pdf). These are occupational, not estimates for the whole state. [CDC National Firefighter Registry](https://www.cdc.gov/niosh/firefighters/registry.html) supports future surveillance.
- **Industrial-release maps:** Iowa [TRI](https://www.iowadnr.gov/environmental-protection/spills-emergency-planning/toxic-release-inventory) is a useful starting point, but reported pounds released or modeled risk are not verified cancer cases attributable to a facility. Site investigations need contaminant/pathway/dose/time evidence.

## The IEC/Harkin report and interpretation

The March 25, 2026 IEC/Harkin **Environmental Risk Factors and Iowa's Cancer Crisis** report is a literature synthesis and advocacy/policy document. It brings relevant studies together and should be considered, but it is not a new statewide exposure-linked causal analysis. An overlap between cancers common in Iowa and cancers ever associated with four exposures is not proof those exposures caused the excess. Conversely, inability to precisely assign the statewide excess does not nullify established hazards or original positive studies. [Release and report](https://www.iaenvironment.org/newsroom/council-news/new-report-finds-ties-between-the-most-common-cancers-in-iowa-and-environmental-risk-factors), [full 88-page report](https://www.iaenvironment.org/webres/File/Environmental%20Risk%20Factors%20and%20Iowa%27s%20Cancer%20Crisis%20-%20Report%281%29.pdf).

## Research gaps to state explicitly

No defensible reviewed evidence supports one exact percent of all Iowa cancers caused by fertilizer, a pesticide, CAFOs or PFAS. Better work needs historical individual residential/water/occupation records, biomarkers, relevant latency, mixtures, diverse populations, cancer subtypes, replication, and careful screening/behavioral adjustment. Current private-well surveillance is incomplete and selected; current county of residence is not lifetime exposure. Household radon/water testing can reduce uncertainty about current exposure but cannot retrospectively assign a cancer's cause.
