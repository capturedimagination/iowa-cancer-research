# Iowa cancer: prevention, screening, access and care

Research checked 22 October 2026. Primary sources and official program pages are prioritized. This memo is research support, not individualized care advice. Dates below distinguish publication from data years.

## Findings that should shape the final report

- Established preventable causes and environmental exposures both warrant attention. National attributable fractions do not identify the reason Iowa ranks second or quantify an individual patient's cause.
- The newest Iowa BRFSS brief is the **2025 survey**, published **October 2026**. It is available from the live HHS BRFSS index, even though some search results still show only 2024. Do not silently label older figures “current.”
- Iowa has a substantial screening access gap by insurance status. Lung screening uptake remains low, but published percentages use different eligibility definitions and denominators.
- HHS now publishes eligibility for free breast, cervical, colorectal and lung screening. The detailed July 2026–June 2027 eligibility page differs from older Care for Yourself copy; verify enrollment with a coordinator before assuming payment.
- HF2635 became law in May 2026, but its cancer screening prior-authorization exemptions have **January 2027 applicability**, not immediate statewide effect. Read the enacted act, not news summaries.

## 1. Modifiable causes: what is established

**National attributable-fraction model.** Islami et al., *CA: A Cancer Journal for Clinicians*, 2024, estimated that 40.0% of incident cancers (excluding nonmelanoma skin cancer) and 44.0% of cancer deaths among U.S. adults aged 30+ in 2019 were attributable to the evaluated potentially modifiable factors. Smoking contributed 19.3% of cases, excess body weight 7.6%, and alcohol 5.4%. These are modeled national population fractions, not Iowa-specific shares; they cannot be added casually because exposures overlap. They do not mean any patient's cancer was preventable, or that all remaining cases are genetic. Source: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/38990124/ (original study; DOI available there).

**Tobacco.** Causal links extend far beyond lung cancer: oral cavity/pharynx, larynx, esophagus, bladder, kidney, liver, stomach, pancreas, colorectal and cervical cancer, plus acute myeloid leukemia. Quitting is useful even after cancer diagnosis, improving treatment and other health outcomes. Current low smoking prevalence does not erase cancers caused by decades of past smoking. Sources: NCI risk overview https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/tobacco ; NCI cessation fact sheet https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/tobacco/cessation-fact-sheet .

**Alcohol.** The 2025 U.S. Surgeon General advisory describes a causal link with at least seven cancer sites: breast, colorectum, liver, esophagus, mouth, throat and larynx. The relevant carcinogen is alcohol consumption regardless of beer/wine/liquor branding. Risk rises with consumption; some cancer risks increase at approximately one drink a day or less. Reducing consumption lowers risk, and not drinking minimizes alcohol-related risk. A high incidence of “alcohol-related cancer sites” does not mean every case at those sites was caused by alcohol. Source: https://www.hhs.gov/sites/default/files/oash-alcohol-cancer-risk.pdf .

**Body weight, diet and activity.** Excess body fat is linked to at least 13 cancer types, including postmenopausal breast, endometrial, colorectal, kidney, liver and pancreas. Mechanisms include hormones, insulin signaling and chronic inflammation. Physical activity and a healthy dietary pattern can reduce risk and help control weight; do not claim that a particular diet or weight-loss drug is established universal cancer prevention. Source: NCI https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/obesity/obesity-fact-sheet .

**UV.** Sun and indoor tanning expose people to carcinogenic ultraviolet radiation. Iowa HHS identifies UV as the cause of most melanomas. Outdoor work, sunburns, tanning and fair-skin susceptibility are relevant; melanoma rates are not proof of contaminated water. Practical prevention includes shade, protective clothing and sunscreen, and avoiding tanning beds. Source: https://hhs.iowa.gov/about/data-reports/cancer/melanoma-skin-data .

**HPV and hepatitis.** Vaccination prevents infections that can later cause cancer. HPV vaccination prevents new infection, not treatment of an existing infection or tumor, and does not replace cervical screening. CDC's live HPV guidance recommends routine vaccination at 11–12 (may begin at 9), catch-up through 26, and discussion for some adults 27–45. Because vaccine policy is actively changing, link current guidance instead of freezing dose counts into a long-lived report. Sources: https://www.cdc.gov/hpv/hcp/vaccination-considerations/ ; https://www.cdc.gov/hepatitis-b/prevention/index.html . Chronic HBV can cause cirrhosis and liver cancer. Hepatitis C detection and treatment are also important, though no HCV vaccine exists.

**Inherited susceptibility.** NCI estimates 5–10% of cancers arise from harmful inherited genetic changes. Family clustering may also reflect shared behaviors/environment. Multiple relatives with related cancers, unusually early diagnoses, multiple primary tumors, ovarian cancer or male breast cancer can merit genetic risk evaluation. Inherited risk does not rule out environmental contributions and vice versa. Source: https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/genetics/genetic-testing-fact-sheet .

## 2. Iowa risk-factor measurements: carefully matched periods

**Newest Iowa survey (2025, published October 2026).** Current adult cigarette smoking: **11.7%** (95% CI 10.9–12.5; pp.40–41). Past-month binge drinking: **17.8%** (16.8–18.8; pp.34–35). Obesity (BMI >=30): **36.9%** (pp.32–33). Smoking is highly unequal: 30.8% among household incomes under $15,000 versus 6.6% at $100,000+, and 19.3% among less-than-high-school versus 4.4% among college graduates. Self-report and survey uncertainty apply. These are weighted crude estimates, not age-adjusted Iowa-US comparisons. Source: https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/19634/download?inline= ; discoverable from https://hhs.iowa.gov/about/data-reports/brfss .

**Matched Iowa-US comparison (2024 BRFSS, age-adjusted).** HHS's February 2026 System Snapshot reports adult binge drinking **22.1% Iowa vs 16.7% U.S.**, and obesity **36.5% vs 34.0%**. These age-adjusted 2024 estimates cannot be directly trended against the crude 2025 values above. Source: https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/15373/download?inline= pp.2–3. (The same snapshot has older cancer-rate periods and should not displace the newer registry figures.)

**HPV vaccine uptake (2025 NIS-Teen, September 3, 2026 publication).** Among Iowa adolescents 13–17, **71.4%** (65.8–76.5) had >=1 HPV dose and **60.4%** (54.4–66.0) were up to date, compared with **77.7%** and **63.4%** nationally. Point estimates differ; do not call Iowa's lower completion statistically significant without a formal comparison. NIS-Teen estimates differ from Iowa's IRIS administrative assessments because age ranges, denominators and methods differ. Source: CDC MMWR Table 3 https://www.cdc.gov/mmwr/volumes/75/wr/mm7534a1.htm .

## 3. Screening: verified final USPSTF recommendations

These apply to asymptomatic people in the stated risk group. A symptom requires diagnostic evaluation, regardless of screening age or recent normal screening. Family history, prior polyps/cancer, genetic syndromes, immunocompromise and prior chest radiation may change the plan. Benefits must be balanced against false positives, complications and overdiagnosis.

| Site | Final USPSTF guidance checked 22 Oct 2026 | Source |
|---|---|---|
| Breast | Mammography every 2 years ages 40–74. Evidence insufficient for routine screening 75+ or supplemental ultrasound/MRI solely for dense breasts after a negative mammogram; individual risk may justify other care. Final 30 Apr 2024. | https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/breast-cancer-screening |
| Colorectal | Screen ages 45–75; selectively 76–85 based on health, past screening and preferences. Options include annual FIT, stool DNA-FIT every 1–3 years, colonoscopy every 10 years, CT colonography every 5 years and specified sigmoidoscopy strategies. Abnormal stool testing needs colonoscopy. Final 18 May 2021. | https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/colorectal-cancer-screening |
| Lung | Annual LDCT ages 50–80 with >=20 pack-years who currently smoke or quit within 15 years. Stop at 15 years since quitting or when health limits life expectancy or ability/willingness for curative lung surgery. Chest X-ray is not an equivalent screening test. Final 9 Mar 2021. | https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/lung-cancer-screening |
| Cervical | Ages 21–29: cytology (Pap) every 3 years. Ages 30–65: Pap every 3 years, primary high-risk HPV every 5 years, or cotesting every 5 years. Applies to people with a cervix; exceptions for high risk. Stop after 65 only if adequate prior screening and not high risk. Current final is 21 Aug 2018; an update is in progress, not final. | https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/cervical-cancer-screening |
| Prostate | Ages 55–69: individualized PSA decision after discussion of modest benefit and harms. Recommends against routine PSA screening at 70+. Current final 8 May 2018, update in progress. | https://www.uspreventiveservicestaskforce.org/uspstf/recommendation/prostate-cancer-screening |

**Guideline difference worth preserving:** ACS recommends annual LDCT ages 50–80 and >=20 pack-years for people who smoke or used to smoke without USPSTF's 15-year-since-quitting cutoff. Eligibility and insurance payment are not identical. https://www.cancer.org/cancer/types/lung-cancer/detection-diagnosis-staging/detection.html .

## 4. Actual Iowa screening and disparities

Iowa 2024 BRFSS (published October 2025): mammogram in past 2 years among women **40+**: **73.7%** (71.8–75.7). This denominator includes 75+ and must not be compared blindly with 40–74 measures. Among women 40+, mammography was 74.7% insured vs 50.7% uninsured; 83.1% college graduates vs 41.2% less-than-high-school (p.52). Colorectal screening meeting recommended intervals, ages **45–75**: **71.7%** (70.0–73.4), 73.1% insured vs **33.5% uninsured** (pp.53–54). Lung: **20.1%** (16.3–23.9) reported a past-year CAT/CT (pp.55–56). Its description/guide uses inconsistent age/history language, so avoid equating it precisely with USPSTF-eligible uptake. Local downloaded source: `sources/Iowa-BRFSS-2024.pdf`. Official URLs: https://hhs.iowa.gov/media/17444/download and https://publications.iowa.gov/54375/1/2024%20BRFSS%20Survey%20Findings%20Brief_Final.pdf .

ACS's 2026 prevention tables offer a cleaner eligibility comparison: 2024 Iowa lung screening **21% among USPSTF-eligible** versus **17% among ACS-eligible**, because ACS's eligible pool is wider; U.S. state median 23% / 18% respectively (Table 6H). Source: https://www.cancer.org/content/dam/cancer-org/research/cancer-facts-and-statistics/cancer-prevention-and-early-detection-facts-and-figures/2026-cped-files/cped-2026-tables-and-figures.pdf . This is primary ACS analysis of BRFSS; it is not a count of completed exams from ACR's registry.

A 2026 Iowa mixed-methods original study combined 2015–2019 registry burden with rural-serving stakeholder interviews in March–May 2023 (14 participants). It found rural excesses in colorectal/bladder/leukemia incidence and colorectal mortality, plus cancer-specific survival differences. Interview themes were long travel, difficult cross-county transit, limited local services and workforce, waiting times and information needs. This demonstrates practical access barriers but does not measure 2026 waiting times or prove rural residence independently causes all differences. Source: Nash et al., *Cancer Causes & Control*, Apr 15 2026, DOI10.1007/s10552-026-02166-9 https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13079507/ .

## 5. Iowa help and care resources

**Free screening eligibility.** Detailed HHS page, dated table July 1, 2026–June 30, 2027: breast generally women 40–64, cervical 21–64; uninsured/underinsured and <=250% FPL. Younger symptomatic/high-risk and some older people without Medicare Part B may qualify. Colorectal ages 45–75: uninsured, no income restrictions listed. Lung ages 50–80: uninsured, >=20 pack-years/current or quit within 15 years, no income restriction listed; address eligibility matters and Polk, Black Hawk and Scott counties excluded. The colorectal FAQ excludes diagnosed/suspected FAP/HNPCC and inflammatory bowel disease despite broader wording elsewhere about increased risk. **Call 866-339-7909 to resolve eligibility before services.** https://hhs.iowa.gov/health-prevention/cancer/cancer-services-participant-eligibility .

Care for Yourself provides navigation and screening/diagnostic services; treatment funding is separate. Its older general page lists 21–64 and different monthly dollars, so avoid treating both as consistent. https://hhs.iowa.gov/health-prevention/cancer/cfy . A page titled `cancer-new-page` contains unfinished link/funding placeholders; do not quote its unfinished funding descriptions as settled program policy.

**Quitline Iowa:** free tobacco quitting support; 1-800-QUIT-NOW (784-8669), text START to 300500, https://www.quitlineiowa.org/ . HHS confirms these routes on the eligibility page. Youth 13–17 can use My Life My Quit, https://mylifemyquit.com/ .

**Specialty care and trials:** Holden at University of Iowa is Iowa's only NCI-designated Comprehensive Cancer Center; the NCI directory verifies designation and phase1/cellular therapy capability. https://cancercenters.cancer.gov/cancer-centers/holden-comprehensive-cancer-center . Open cancer trial finder and patient information: https://uihc.org/cancer/cancer-clinical-trials . Eligibility depends on diagnosis, treatment history and health; investigational treatment is not guaranteed to work.

**Community delivery:** Mission Cancer + Blood joined UI Health Care after assets purchased Dec31 2024 (announcement Jan6 2025); >20 community cancer clinics offer local oncology, infusions, labs, trials and tumor boards. This describes the network, not proof that every community has every modality. https://uihealthcare.org/press-release/mission-cancer-blood-now-officially-part-university-iowa-health-care .

**Financial help:** UI Health Care's 2026 financial assistance page lists Iowa residency, income <=350% federal poverty guidelines, income verification and Medicaid eligibility process among requirements. Eligibility is individualized; do not promise free treatment. Contacts: 1-866-393-4605 for billing/assistance; Mission 515-282-3336. https://uihc.org/cancer/financial-and-billing-assistance .

**Travel/lodging:** ACS Hope Lodge in Iowa City offers eligible patients/caregivers traveling for outpatient cancer care free lodging, subject to program acceptance/availability; ACS 1-800-227-2345. https://www.cancer.org/support-programs-and-services/patient-lodging/hope-lodge.html . Local support directory: https://www.missioncancerfoundation.org/resource-guide/ . Registry's 99-counties guide (Sept2025): https://shri.public-health.uiowa.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/10/Resource-Guide-99-Counties-09.22.25.pdf . Patient navigation/social work can connect financial, transportation, psychosocial, nutrition and survivorship support.

**State planning:** Iowa Cancer Consortium's 2023–2027 plan covers equity, prevention, screening, diagnosis/therapy, survivorship and end-of-life care. https://canceriowa.org/iowa-cancer-plan/ . It is a collaborative action framework, not a claim all goals have been achieved.

## 6. Current policy: dates and scope matter

**HF2635 / 2026 Acts ch1087.** Bill history verifies signing May13 2026 and general effective date July1 2026. But enacted **section7** (specified commercial/public employee plans) and **sections9/11** (medical assistance/Hawki) make cancer-screening prior-authorization exemptions applicable to covered plans/contracts and requests **on/after January1 2027**. Screening must be provider-recommended under specified NCCN guidance. This does not waive all costs, cover every test, or apply universally to all insurance. As of the research date, do not tell patients prior authorization is already banned statewide. Sources: bill history https://www.legis.iowa.gov/legislation/billTracking/billHistory?billName=HF2635&ga=91 ; enacted act https://www.legis.iowa.gov/docs/publications/iactc/91.2/CH1087.pdf pp.4–7. The official final fiscal analysis expressly discusses Medicaid/Hawki, contrary to some early news reporting.

**Rural Health Transformation / Healthy Hometowns.** CMS announcement October9 2026 describes **$209m overall FY2026 Iowa rural health funding**, including cancer prevention/screening, workforce, EMS, maternal care, infrastructure and information sharing. It is not $209m exclusively for cancer. Announced investment and early installations are not demonstrated reductions in cancer outcomes. https://www.cms.gov/newsroom/press-releases/trump-administration-announces-209-million-expand-advanced-medical-care-maternal-telehealth-cancer .

## 7. Practical priorities supported by the evidence

Stop tobacco and avoid secondhand smoke; reduce alcohol; support sustainable healthy weight, activity and diet; prevent UV exposure; complete indicated HPV/HBV vaccination; use risk-appropriate screening and ensure abnormal results receive follow-up; investigate inherited risk when warranted; address travel, insurance and treatment affordability. Environmental exposure reduction belongs alongside these measures. None eliminates cancer risk or assigns blame to patients.
